I used to think the first &euro;1,000,000 in revenue would feel like an
arrival. It didn't. It felt like the moment the questions got harder, not
easier — because now there was something real to protect, and protecting it
turned out to be a completely different skill than building it.

## Revenue is not the number that matters

Nobody warns you how little a big top-line number tells you about whether
your business is actually healthy. The number that changed how I slept was
never revenue — it was how many months I could survive if my biggest customer
left tomorrow.

## The milestone changes your risk tolerance, quietly

Before the milestone, every decision felt like upside: what's the best case if
this works? After it, I noticed myself asking a different question without
meaning to: what's the worst case if this breaks something I've already
built? That shift is natural, and it's also dangerous if you don't name it —
it's how founders who once moved fast start moving cautiously for the wrong
reasons.

## What actually mattered more than the number

- **Who I built it with.** The milestone belonged to a small group of people,
  not to me alone, and treating it that way mattered more than I expected.
- **What I learned about my own limits.** Money problems became
  people-and-systems problems long before they became money problems again.
- **How boring the "secret" turned out to be.** Consistency, unglamorous
  follow-through, and saying no to good-enough opportunities to protect great
  ones.

If you're chasing a number like this one, chase it. Just don't expect it to
answer the questions you think it will. It mostly just changes which
questions you're allowed to ask next.
